1. What was designed

The bracket arm is filled with a graded lattice whose strut widths follow an optimised 4×2 control field (thicker near the clamped wall, thinner toward the tip). The optimiser minimised mass subject to a reliability target on the batch distribution of a recycled feedstock, not on a single nominal material. Compared with the solid plate, the robust design removes 58% of the printed mass; compared with a uniform lattice sized for the same reliability it saves 14%.

Mass basis: the headline is the deterministic union-volume estimate (Halton 60k union estimate) of the printed struts + pad. The analytic strut-sum is 50.8 g, a conservative upper bound (9.5% higher on this design); the old 25% audit figure was traced to a union-sampler bug and is retired.

1b. What changed in v0.3 (review response)

  • Mass basis fixed: headline design masses are printed union estimates (Halton 60k samples); the analytic strut-sum (50.8 g) is a conservative upper bound (+9.5%). The old 25% figure came from a validator sampler bug and is retired.
  • Printability is a constraint: neighbour-width ratio ≤ 1.5 and slenderness ≤ 30 are enforced in the optimiser; shipped design: ratio 1.38, slenderness 21.9.
  • Known-lot passports keep measurement uncertainty: default 12% measurement CV on E and sy; a single coupon is not the true lot mean.
  • Lot-specific resize: the passenger recipe uses 8 width multipliers from a second optimisation on the measured lot, not one global scale (the global equivalent is shown for slicer extrusion settings).
  • Feasibility gate: before Design, the engine checks whether the bracket/lattice family can meet the target at the maximum printable width. This material: feasible (minimum achievable Pf 0.00e+00).
  • Sustained-load advisory (creep / compression bound): Ereq × 2.0, σreq / 0.8, deflection × 2.0; bound Pf 1.00, deflection bound 4.0 mm. This static design is not qualified for sustained load; the bound is advisory, not the design allowable.
  • Family scope: "best of this bracket/lattice family" — not a general topology optimiser. A pre-registered coupon plan is in validation/COUPON_TEST_PLAN.md; no physical test has been run.

3. Why the part is reliable (and where it is not)

For a single-material frame, internal forces are independent of the elastic modulus, so each geometry imposes two material demands: a yield-strength demand σreq and a stiffness demand Ereq (from the deflection limit and from Euler buckling of compression struts). A lot fails if either material property falls below demand. This makes the failure probability a closed-form bivariate lognormal integral over the lot distribution.

Safety-margin distribution over 60,000 simulated lots (the closed-form Pf is 1.00%; Monte-Carlo adds sampling noise). Values below 1.0 are failures; P5 marks the weakest 5% of lots.
Failure-mode balance: the marginal probability that the lot's strength is insufficient vs the marginal probability that its stiffness is insufficient. The joint Pf is smaller than the sum because a lot can be weak in both properties at once. The dominant failure contributor is stiffness buckling (exactly the property to control in the supply chain); the demand closest to capacity is stiffness buckling (strength demand at 41% of the lot mean, stiffness demand at 80%).

4. Batch passport: compensating the lot you actually received

Instead of rejecting weak lots, the same software computes how much the internal geometry must be scaled for that specific lot to keep the reliability target. In v0.3 the recipe is not one global scale: the engine re-optimises the eight width controls on the measured lot (8 width multipliers, reported in the app) and keeps a 12% measurement uncertainty in Pf; the table shows the global-equivalent scale for a slicer's extrusion-multiplier setting. 5. Verification evidence (why you can trust the model at all)

Code verification passed in this build: single-element cantilever FE matches the Euler–Bernoulli closed form to machine precision (relative error < 1e-15); the bivariate-normal failure integral matches SciPy's reference to 2e-15; the closed-form Pf matches an independent Monte-Carlo estimate (analytic 0.0100 vs MC 0.0106 ± 0.0004); mass and stiffness scale linearly as expected; the Python and JavaScript implementations are cross-checked against identical reference fixtures. What is not verified: any physical specimen — no laboratory was available. The material distributions are literature-derived and provisional; the mechanics are a linear-elastic plane-frame idealisation.

Model credibility follows the logic of ASME V&V 10/20/40 (verification, validation, uncertainty quantification). Claim level of this report: L0–L1 (equations and numerics verified) + partial L2 (literature-calibrated inputs with UQ); L3 literature reproduction is planned, L4–L6 validation/certification are absent because no physical testing was performed. See research/05-validation-without-physical-testing.md for the full claim ladder and the minimum experimental programme that would upgrade these results.

6. Limitations (read before using any number)

  • In-silico only; no experimental validation was possible in this project.
  • Linear-elastic plane-frame idealisation; no plasticity, creep or fatigue.
  • Euler buckling with pinned-pinned effective length (k=1), pure axial load (no combined axial-bending buckling); rigid joints are not modelled explicitly.
  • Strut mass sums element volumes without subtracting material overlap at nodes; this is conservative (over-estimates mass) for thick struts.
  • Batch-uniform material inside a part; no spatial scatter or print defects.
  • Literature-derived feedstock distributions (provisional v0.1).
  • Not a certification tool. Use as decision support and validate before production.

Variability-suppression levers deliberately not active in v0.1 (so no number here can be misread as one): (i) compliance dilution with a second phase — the nacre-style mechanism — needs a two-phase material model and costs roughly 2.4× mass to recover stiffness; (ii) spatial averaging of within-lot scatter needs a spatial correlation length and per-element material fields; (iii) batch mixing needs a multi-lot blending model. The published 93 % PNAS variability-reduction result is relative to their modelled 26 % introduced variability (their measured feedstock was 18 % → 4 %, i.e. a 78 % reduction) and is achieved by a two-phase composite whose strength is adhesive-limited — it does not transfer to a single-material printed bracket. AnyRB v0.1 changes the decision and the mean demand; it does not claim to reduce the material's own CV.
